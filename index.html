Big AND Abs — How That Actually Works

These two goals pull against each other, so here is the honest version before you start.

  • You are already lean. At 6'0" and 166 lb you are not carrying much fat. For you, abs are not hiding under a layer of fat — the abs themselves are just small. Abs are muscles. You have to build them like chest and arms, which is why this plan now has a real weighted core block instead of a token set of crunches.
  • Size comes from a surplus. You cannot gain a lot of muscle while eating at maintenance. Calories go up to 3,400–3,600 and target gain goes to 0.5–0.75 lb/week — faster than the old plan, still controlled.
  • Faster is not better. Past ~0.75 lb/week your body cannot build muscle any quicker, so the extra is fat — and fat over the stomach is the one thing that actually does hide abs. Gaining 2 lb a week for a month means buying yourself a cut you did not need.
  • The waist is your dashboard. Chest and arms up, waist roughly flat = perfect. Waist climbing as fast as your arms = surplus too big, pull back 200 kcal.
  • You cannot spot-reduce. Doing 300 crunches does not burn stomach fat. Crunches build the muscle; food controls the fat. Both matter, neither replaces the other.
  • Timeline is honest, not hype. Ab strength and thickness in 6–10 weeks. Real upper-body size in 3–6 months. "Big" is a 2–4 year project, and starting at 17 with good sleep and real food is the best possible position to start from.
Best-case realistic first year: roughly 15–25 lb of mostly muscle, a visibly bigger chest, arms and shoulders, and abs that show at rest. That is a genuinely different body — and it is only reachable if you stop skipping meals.

Two Rules That Shape Everything

Dizziness with repeated head movement. Every exercise here is done seated, supported, or standing still with the head in one steady position. No fast up-and-down head changes, no lying-to-standing repeats, no inverted or bent-over-with-hanging-head positions. Between sets, sit still and stand up slowly.
Knee issues. No heavy barbell back squats, walking lunges, Bulgarian split squats, jumping, burpees, or mountain climbers. Legs are trained with the leg press (controlled depth), machines, and supported hip work — pain-free range only.
This site is an organized training and nutrition plan, not medical advice. Because of the dizziness and the knee history, get both checked by a doctor or athletic trainer — WPI has Health Services on campus — before pushing hard.

The four training days are unchanged. The blue Core Block at the bottom is the new addition for abs — 10–15 minutes tacked onto the end of any three sessions a week (Day 1, Day 2 and Day 4 works well; skipping it on leg day is fine).

Rules for every session

  • Rest 90–180 seconds between sets — full rest on the big presses, shorter on arms.
  • Leave 1–3 reps in reserve on most sets. You should always feel you could do 1–3 more with good form.
  • Warm up 5 minutes on a bike or elliptical (low impact, head steady), then easy shoulder/hip mobility, then 1–2 light sets of the first exercise.
  • Control the weight: about 1–2 seconds up, 2–3 seconds down. No bouncing, no throwing.
  • Stop a set if form breaks, a knee hurts, or the room starts to move. That set is over — that's not failure, that's the plan working.

Nutrition & Diet

You are naturally skinny. Training is only half of it — you will not gain weight without eating more than you burn, consistently, on training days and rest days. These numbers are the bigger, size-focused version: enough surplus to actually grow, small enough that your waist does not run away from you.

That is roughly 400 kcal more than the original plan. If jumping straight there makes you feel stuffed and sick, add 200 kcal now and the other 200 in a week — a surplus you can actually eat every day beats a perfect one you quit after four days.

The Adjustment Rule

  • Weigh yourself 4–7 mornings a week, right after the bathroom, before eating. Use the weekly average, never one single day.
  • Weight flat for 10–14 days? Add 250–300 kcal/day (one shake, or a second scoop of rice and an extra chicken thigh).
  • Gaining faster than ~0.75 lb/week for two weeks? Pull back 200–250 kcal/day. Your body cannot build muscle any faster than that, so the extra is going to your stomach — the one place you least want it.
  • Gaining 0.5–0.75 lb/week? Change nothing. That is 2–3 lb a month, and it is close to the fastest anyone builds real muscle without also getting soft.
  • Check the waist, not just the scale. Measure your waist at the belly button every two weeks. Chest and arms up while the waist holds within about an inch = the surplus is right. Waist up an inch a month = too fast.
  • Don't chase daily swings. Water, salt, and how much food is in you can move the scale 3 lb in a day.

Meal Timing

  • 5–6 eating occasions per day — three real meals plus 2–3 shakes/snacks. At 3,500 kcal, spreading it out is the difference between doable and miserable.
  • Protein at every occasion: 25–40 g each time spreads muscle-building across the whole day.
  • Before training: a carb + protein meal or snack 1–2 hours out. Training on an empty stomach makes dizziness more likely.
  • After training: eat within a couple of hours — a full meal is better than a shake if you can get one.
  • Rest days matter more than you think. Muscle is built on rest days. Eat the same amount.
  • Water: ~3 L/day, more on training days. Dehydration makes lightheadedness worse.

Safety & Recovery

Read this first. Dizziness with repeated head movement and knee pain both deserve an actual medical evaluation — they're not things to "train around" forever. Get seen by a doctor, athletic trainer, or physical therapist. Everything below is how to train sensibly in the meantime, not a diagnosis or treatment.

Avoid Or Modify — Dizziness

The trigger is repeated head movement, so the plan removes the positions that force it.

Avoid

  • Repeated lying-down-to-standing-up transitions between sets
  • Bent-over rows, deadlifts, or good mornings with the head hanging
  • Any inverted or head-below-heart position (decline benches, hanging, inversion)
  • Burpees, mountain climbers, jump training, fast sit-ups / crunch repetitions
  • Fast overhead-to-floor movements, kettlebell swings, sprint intervals
  • Looking up and down repeatedly to check the mirror or your phone mid-set

Do instead

  • Seated, back-supported machines with the head still and eyes forward on one fixed point
  • Stay in one position for the whole set, then rise slowly over 3–5 seconds
  • Rest sitting up, not lying flat — then stand and pause before walking
  • Set up equipment before the set so you're not bending down repeatedly
  • Eat and drink before training; low fuel and dehydration make it worse
  • Train with a friend, or at least where staff can see you, until this is sorted out
About the ab work. This is why the Core Block contains no sit-ups, floor crunches, V-ups or Russian twists — every one of them moves your head up and down or side to side dozens of times per set, which is precisely your trigger. Knee raises, the seated machine crunch, the Pallof press, planks and dead bugs all keep your head either upright and still or resting on a mat, and they build abs better than sit-ups anyway.
If the room starts to spin: stop, sit or hold a stable frame, keep your eyes on one fixed point, breathe, and don't stand until it fully passes. End the session. Don't drive.

Avoid Or Modify — Knees

Avoid

  • Heavy barbell back squats and front squats
  • Walking lunges, reverse lunges, Bulgarian split squats, step-ups
  • Jump squats, box jumps, plyometrics, running, stairs for cardio
  • Deep leg press below a pain-free depth, or knees caving inward
  • Heavy, jerky leg extensions at very short/locked-out angles
  • Letting the knee travel far past the toes under load

Do instead

  • Leg press with a mid-to-high foot placement and controlled, partial depth
  • Machine leg extensions and leg curls with moderate weight and slow tempo
  • Supported hip thrusts / glute bridges — strong glutes take load off the knee
  • Calf raises, which are essentially knee-neutral
  • Only pain-free ranges — shorten the range before you reduce the effort
  • Low-impact cardio only: stationary bike (seat high enough) or elliptical
Mild muscle soreness in the days after training is normal. Sharp pain inside the joint, pain that gets worse set after set, or pain that lingers into the next day is not — reduce the range, reduce the load, or drop the exercise.

Stop And Get Checked If…

  • You faint, nearly faint, or have dizziness that doesn't clear within a few minutes of resting
  • Dizziness comes with a headache, vision changes, ringing ears, slurred speech, or numbness
  • Chest pain or pressure, unusual shortness of breath, or a racing/irregular heartbeat
  • A knee swells, locks, catches, or gives way
  • Any joint pain that persists more than a few days, or any sudden sharp pain during a lift
  • You feel faint every time you stand up — mention it, along with your calorie intake and blood pressure, to the doctor
On campus: WPI Health Services for medical evaluation. If something is severe or sudden — fainting, chest pain, neurological symptoms — that's an emergency, not a wait-and-see.

Recovery

Sleep — the real supplement

Target 8–9 hours a night. At 17 you're still growing; sleep is when growth hormone, recovery, and appetite regulation happen. Consistent bed and wake times beat catching up on weekends. Under-sleeping also makes dizziness and poor lifts far more likely.

Cardio

Low impact only and limited while gaining: 10–20 min, 2–3×/week on a bike or elliptical, easy pace. Walking is unlimited and good for you. No running, stairs, or intervals — both the knees and the head-movement issue say no.

Warm-up (every session)

5 min easy bike/elliptical → arm circles, shoulder rolls, band pull-aparts, easy hip openers (all standing or seated, head steady) → 1–2 light sets of the day's first exercise at ~50% of your working weight.

Optional mobility

After training or on rest days: chest doorway stretch, lat stretch, seated hamstring and calf stretch, gentle hip flexor stretch. 20–30 seconds each, no bouncing, nothing that provokes the knee or requires head-down positions.

Signs Of Overtraining

  • Lifts going backwards for 2+ weeks despite eating enough
  • Constantly sore, joints aching, or soreness lasting 4+ days
  • Trouble sleeping despite feeling exhausted; resting heart rate up
  • Losing appetite, losing weight without trying
  • Getting sick repeatedly, irritable, unmotivated to train
  • Dizziness happening more often than usual
The fix is almost never "push harder." Take 3–5 days easy or run a deload week, sleep more, eat more — then come back and usually hit personal bests.

Progressive Overload

One rule decides whether you grow: the work has to get harder over time. Same weight, same reps, forever = same body, forever.

Double progression — the only method you need

  1. Pick a weight you can do for the bottom of the rep range with good form.
  2. Stay at that weight and add reps session by session until you hit the top of the range on all sets, with good form.
  3. Next session, add the smallest increment available (5 lb on a machine stack, 2.5–5 lb per dumbbell). Your reps will drop back toward the bottom of the range.
  4. Repeat. Forever.

If you miss the bottom of the range

Stay at the same weight next session, or drop it by 5–10% and rebuild. Missing reps once means you were tired; missing them two sessions in a row means the weight is too heavy or you're under-recovered.

Reps in reserve (RIR)

Train 1–3 reps from failure on most sets. Push the last set of an isolation exercise (curls, pushdowns, raises) closer to failure — never on a press where the weight could pin you.

Deload every 6–8 weeks

Or sooner if you're beat up. For one week: keep the same exercises, cut sets roughly in half and use ~60–70% of your normal weights. You come back stronger — this is part of the plan, not a break from it.

Realistic timeline

4–8 weeks: strength climbing fast, some early size, clothes fitting differently.
8–12+ weeks: visible change in chest, shoulders and arms — with consistency and a real calorie surplus. Nothing happens without both.

Progress isn't only weight on the bar. More reps, better control, deeper pain-free range, less rest needed, an extra set — all of it counts as overload.

Adding Size Faster (Safely)

Once the four days feel routine — roughly week 5 onward — these are the levers, in the order worth pulling. Add one at a time and give it three weeks before adding another.

  • 1. Eat the surplus first. More sets on top of not enough food just digs a hole. If your weekly average is not climbing 0.5–0.75 lb, fix the food before touching the training.
  • 2. Add a set to the first exercise of each day. Chest press to 5 sets, cable row to 4, leg press to 4, shoulder press to 4. That is the highest-value extra work in the whole plan.
  • 3. Add a set to one arm exercise per day. Arms recover fast and you are training them twice a week — they can handle it.
  • 4. Rest-pause the last set of arms and delts. Take the final set to 1–2 reps from failure, rest 15 seconds sitting still, then squeeze out 3–5 more reps. Use it on pushdowns, curls and lateral raises only — never on a press.
  • 5. Add the optional exercises. The "optional" items on Day 1, 2 and 4 stop being optional once you have the energy and the food to support them.
  • Do not add a 5th training day. Four hard days plus the core block plus 8–9 hours of sleep beats five rushed days, and a fifth day is the fastest way to make the dizziness and the knee worse.
If two lifts stall at once, sleep drops, or the dizziness shows up more often, you added too much. Go back to the previous volume for two weeks — that is information, not failure.
